Quick Recap: What RU Already Covers

These points came up again this week but RU already has solid coverage — don't re-teach them as new, just reinforce them:

  • Suppressed additional fees (Week 12/14): adding an income type like pet fee income or application fee income as an additional management fee and marking it suppressed hides it from the owner statement while still tracking it internally. Week 12's real-world notes already walk through a live example (a Pet Fee Income GL account that was never wired into fee settings after a platform migration).
  • Vacant-pending fee combining (Week 12): Rentvine pulls the fee bill from the vacant-property setting only and does not automatically add the leased minimum fee on top — the two have to be manually totaled into one bill when a portfolio's policy charges both.
  • Property-level fee settings (Week 12): management fee percentages live at the property level, not the unit level, which matters when commercial suites were built as separate properties instead of units.
  • Two-bank-account cap (Week 17): each portfolio is capped at two bank accounts — rental trust and security deposit. There is no third account for something like non-refundable fees; route it through the trust account and manually transfer if needed.
  • The Diagnostics tab (Week 14): already covered in depth, including reading the sign on a Suppressed Fee Balance Mismatch (positive = owed to the management company, negative = owed back to the owner).
  • Post Management Fees vs. Bulk Management Fees (Week 12): Bulk Management Fees is a separate one-time-charge tool (like an annual portal activation fee), not a shortcut for clearing the normal unbilled management fee queue.
  • Manual ledger adjustments (Week 10): already discouraged in favor of Other Receipt or Other Payment, since manual adjustments throw off reconciliation.

Management Fee % Is Locked at Charge Creation

This one trips up new admins constantly: changing a portfolio or property's management fee percentage in settings is not retroactive. Any charge that already exists was created using whatever percentage was in effect at that moment, and it stays that way permanently — updating the setting only affects charges created after the change.

If a customer needs an existing charge to reflect a new percentage, the only path is to void the original charge and recreate it under the new setting. There's no bulk "reprocess at new rate" tool.

Changing the fee percentage in settings only affects new charges going forward. If you need last month's charge to reflect the new rate, we'll need to void it and recreate it — the setting change alone won't touch anything that's already posted.

GL Account Discipline: Income vs. Expense

A recurring theme across the accounting-heavy calls this week: lease-level charges need to post to income-type GL accounts, and bills need to post to expense-type GL accounts. A common naming pattern makes the distinction obvious once you know to look for it — for example, "painting income" (charged to a tenant or owner) versus "painting expense" (paid to a vendor).

When both sides accidentally use the same account type, the two entries can net to zero on reports instead of showing the true inflow and outflow. A related and even more damaging mistake: using a clearing or liability account instead of a proper expense account on a bill. That distorts the portfolio's real balance rather than just muddying one report.

Before you save that bill, check the GL account type — bills need an expense account, not an income account and not a clearing or liability account. If both sides of a transaction use the same account type, your reports will show it as a wash even though real money moved.

Coding Reimbursements Correctly

Owner receipts or "other receipts" that represent a reimbursement — a vendor refund, or a refund from the management company itself — should not be coded to an income account. Code them to an offsetting account instead: either an owner contributions account, or back to the original expense account the reimbursement is correcting.

Coding a reimbursement as income overstates the portfolio's actual income for the period and can throw off owner statements and 1099 reporting downstream.

Overdraft Override: The $0 Hard Stop

RU already teaches that Overdraft Override shouldn't be used to push a portfolio negative. Here's the mechanical detail behind that warning: setting the override amount to exactly $0 is what hard-blocks any bill payment that would drive the portfolio balance negative. It's the strict, no-exceptions setting.

Sometimes a temporary override is legitimately needed — for example, funds are confirmed to be arriving the next business day and a bill needs to go out today. That's fine as a short-term exception, but the override should always be reset back to $0 immediately afterward. Trust accounts commingle funds across owners, so leaving a nonzero override in place is what actually creates the cross-owner borrowing problem RU already warns about.

If you need a one-time exception because funds are landing tomorrow, that's fine — but the moment that bill is paid, set the override back to $0. Leaving it open is how one owner's trust funds end up covering another owner's bill.

E-Checks, Forte, and the Can't-Void Rule

Checks and "other payment" types can be voided and re-recorded if something goes wrong. Electronic checks processed through Forte cannot — once an e-check payment is submitted, it's final. The same applies to bills paid via automated e-check: there's no direct void, only a return or a journal entry to correct it after the fact.

This means the moment to catch a mistake is before submitting, not after. Double-check the payee, amount, and bank account on any Forte e-check before you send it.

Separately, outbound electronic-check payments through Forte carry a $0.99-per-transaction fee that's auto-debited. NACHA files are the manual alternative: instead of Forte handling the transaction electronically, you download a NACHA file from Rentvine and upload it to the bank yourself.

Once you hit submit on a Forte e-check, that's it — it can't be voided. Take the extra ten seconds to confirm the amount and payee before you send it, not after.

Late Fees: Automate, Then Void What You Waive

The recommended pattern for late fees is to let the system's automation post them on schedule, then void the specific late fees you want to waive — for example, giving grace for a holiday or weekend rent due date. That's a cleaner approach than disabling automation altogether and entering late fees manually, which reintroduces the risk of forgetting one or getting the amount wrong.

Monthly Close Order

A confirmed, repeatable monthly close sequence: post management fees, then pay bills, then print statements. Statements should always be generated for the previous period, not the current one still in progress.

StepAction
1Post management fees
2Pay bills
3Print/publish owner statements (for the previous period)

RUBS: Ratio Utility Billing System

RUBS came up in the context of a shared water bill for a 4-unit building. Instead of trying to sub-meter or guess an individual unit's usage, RUBS splits one shared utility bill evenly across the units (or tenants) it serves. Depending on how a property is set up, that produces either tenant-facing charges (if the cost is billed back to residents) or an owner-side lease charge (if the owner is absorbing the utility cost instead of passing it through).

Vacant-Unit Utility Bills

When a unit is vacant, its utility bill can be logged directly against that vacant unit rather than being divided across the occupied units in the portfolio. It's still paid out of the portfolio's overall operating balance — there's no need to allocate a vacant unit's utility cost across other tenants just because it doesn't have a resident to bill.

Beginning Balances and the Migration Artifact

New customers sometimes look at their opening owner statement and question a lump-sum beginning or opening balance they don't recognize line by line. In most cases that's not a bug — it's the balance-forward import carrying over a single consolidated number from the customer's previous software (Buildium was the example that came up this week). Weeks 20 and 21 already cover the Balance Forward Workbook mechanics in depth; this is simply a good talking point to have ready when a confused or skeptical new owner asks about it.

That beginning balance is a single number carried over from your old software during onboarding — it's a starting point, not a transaction we're hiding detail on. Everything from this point forward will show full detail.

Separate Portfolios for Separate Bank Accounts

An owner who wants different properties paid out to different bank accounts can't do that inside a single portfolio — that requires separate portfolios, one per bank account relationship. Owners with more than one portfolio switch between them from a 3-dot menu inside the owner portal.

One thing worth confirming with owners moving between portfolios or coming from another platform: even though the underlying accounting is portfolio-based, owner statements still show property-level income, expense, and net breakdowns. Splitting into multiple portfolios for banking purposes doesn't mean losing property-level visibility in reporting.

Closing

Most of what surfaces on real onboarding and QA calls isn't a new feature to learn — it's a timing rule, a GL habit, or a one-sentence caveat that only becomes obvious after a customer hits it. Fee percentage timing, GL account discipline, the Overdraft Override $0 stop, e-check voiding limits, and monthly close order are exactly that kind of detail: small, easy to state clearly once you know it, and easy to miss otherwise.
