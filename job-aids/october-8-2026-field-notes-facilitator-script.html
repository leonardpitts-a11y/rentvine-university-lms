Introduction

Today's notes come from one busy day of calls — and the common thread is finality and defaults: which actions in Rentvine can't be undone (submitted e-checks, processed screenings during a credit freeze), which defaults new users miss (deposit refunds default to check, recurring charges only post on active leases), and which fixes are available when something lands wrong (post-date edits, profile deactivation, deleting an erroneous recurring credit).

E-Check Finality: The $1,845 Overpayment

On the Southern Choice Q/A call, a vendor payee received an e-check payout of $1,845 when the intended amount was $1,445. The client had already contacted support; the key teaching point is what the trainer said about the processor: once an e-check has been submitted through Forte, direct cancellation options are gone. From there it becomes a support/processor conversation, not a button in the UI.

Treat every e-check submission like dropping an envelope in a mailbox. Before you post, that's your window to catch the amount — after you post, there is no cancel button, only a support conversation with the processor.

Just as important is the root cause the team found: a non-refundable inspection fee and a non-refundable pet fee had been miscategorized as other income instead of being suppressed. That misclassification inflated the portfolio's balance, which is why the payout came out oversized. The lesson pairs with the fee-mechanics material: non-refundable fee suppression isn't cosmetic — it directly protects the owner-payout math.

When a payout is mysteriously too big, don't start at the payment — start at what fed the balance. Here, two non-refundable fees that should have been suppressed showed up as other income, and the distribution faithfully paid out money that was never the owner's to receive.

What Refunds Rentvine Can (and Can't) Do

From the internal Morning Training, a clean statement of the refund boundaries that trips up property managers:

  • Rentvine supports exactly two tenant refund paths: ACH, or a manual refund processed outside the system recorded as an "other" payment type.
  • There is no direct credit or debit card refund. Selecting "credit/debit card" on an other-payment refund is only a label describing what you did outside the system — it does not process a card refund.
  • Because a manual refund happens outside the system, it cannot display the last four digits of any card — that data never passed through Rentvine.
  • The in-system ACH tenant refund lives in the move-out flow (step four) — that's the only place it exists.
If a client insists on refunding "back to the card," the honest answer is: the system can't do that. We can refund by ACH inside the move-out flow, or they can process the card refund with their own merchant tools outside the system and record it here as an other payment — which is also why no last-four will ever display on it.

Move-Out Mechanics: Defaults, MFA & Portal Shutoff

The Southern Choice call included a full live move-out. Three details worth adding to the Week 15 walkthrough:

  • The security deposit refund defaults to check during the move-out workflow; e-check is the alternative if selected. The client in this case writes and mails a physical check from the security deposit bank account — a perfectly supported pattern.
  • Submitting the move-out requires a multi-factor authentication text code — warn trainees so the prompt doesn't surprise them mid-flow.
  • Remember to disable the tenant's portal access as part of completion so the former tenant can't keep logging in.
The move-out flow will stop you for a text verification code before it finalizes — that's expected, not an error. And before you call it done: forwarding address confirmed, refund method chosen, portal access switched off.

The Ledger Discrepancy Toolkit

Two complementary diagnostic moves were demonstrated on the Southern Choice call, both worth teaching as standard practice:

1. The zero-point trace

For a portfolio balance that "looks wrong": open the portfolio ledger, set the report view to all time, find the last date the account zeroed out, then click through every increase and decrease after that date, opening the underlying receipt or payment for each until the discrepancy shows itself.

Every balance mystery has a birthday. Find the last day the ledger sat at zero, and everything you need to explain is between that day and today — walk the increases and decreases one by one.

2. The post-date edit

A real $175 discrepancy traced to a ceiling-fan repair bill: the payment was created September 12 but actually paid October 3, so the ledger showed a negative balance in the wrong period. The fix: open the portfolio ledger, select the decrease entry, green actions → edit, and correct the post date to when the payment really happened.

3. Find the bill before you re-enter it

To avoid duplicate bill entry, search existing bills first: Accounting → Money Out → Bills, then search by invoice/reference number or exact amount — or open the specific portfolio's bills tab and clear the posting-date filter to see everything tied to that portfolio.

Before you key in any vendor invoice, spend ten seconds searching the invoice number or the amount. A duplicate bill takes seconds to create and a reconciliation cycle to unwind.

One more framing from the same call, useful when owners ask "where's my money?": a portfolio's held-back amounts are the sum of accounts payable (unpaid bills including lease, convenience and management fees), reserves, work order estimates, and unsettled or undeposited receipts. And bills and invoices never age out — they stay in the system indefinitely, even after being paid, unless voided.

Duplicate Tenant Profiles & Portal Logins

From the Lepi & Associates Q/A, two portal-login failure modes with clean fixes:

  • No verification code arriving: a tenant won't receive an email verification code if they haven't yet registered or accepted their portal invitation. Check invite status before troubleshooting email delivery.
  • Tenant logs in and "sees nothing": in the real case, the tenant had two profiles and only one was connected to the lease — she was logging into the other one. The fix: make sure the lease is on the profile that has portal access, then remove the duplicate.

The disposal rule for a duplicate profile: remove the invitation first; then delete it outright if it has no accounting transactions attached — otherwise deactivate it. The same rule covered deleting a test internal-technician profile: unassign it from any active work orders, remove the invite, then delete or deactivate.

"I can log in but there's nothing there" almost always means two profiles and the lease is on the other one. Fix the linkage first, then clean up: delete the duplicate if it's never touched accounting, deactivate it if it has.

Recurring Charges, Recurring Credits & Autopay Collisions

Three ledger surprises from the same call, all involving recurring items:

  • Recurring charges only post on active leases. A "missing" recurring charge traced to a lease stuck in pending — caused by a property-setup issue where a main house and a rear apartment shared one property record. Label the units distinctly, fix the lease status, and the charges post.
  • A recurring credit is not a recurring charge. A support rep had accidentally added a recurring credit instead of a recurring charge, which generated duplicate credits on the tenant ledger (an unexpected $1,500 credit plus a duplicate prepayment). The fix was deleting the erroneous recurring credit. When a tenant ledger shows credits nobody intended, check the recurring items list first.
  • Autopay plus a manual payment equals a double payment. A tenant set up autopay for the 5th and also paid manually the same day. This is tenant-side misconfiguration, not a system bug — but it ends in a refund conversation, so teach clients to warn tenants when enabling autopay.
When a charge didn't post, check the lease status before anything else — recurring charges wait politely for a lease to be active. And when a mystery credit appears, open the recurring items list: a recurring credit added by mistake will keep manufacturing new credits until someone deletes it.

Internal Maintenance: The Labor & Materials Tab

The Lepi call surfaced several internal-maintenance mechanics that aren't obvious from the UI:

  • The Labor & Materials tab on a work order is hidden until an internal technician is assigned — if you can't find it, check the assignment first.
  • Labor hours and material costs do not flow automatically onto owner bills — today they must be calculated manually when billing. The product may automate this later, but don't promise it.
  • The price book is reference-only: it stores labor hours and material pricing for historical lookup, not for automated billing calculations.
  • An exclusive third-party vendor can be configured to function like an internal maintenance company with checks routed to them — it works, but the configuration needs careful handling.
The Labor & Materials tab plays hide-and-seek: no internal technician assigned, no tab. And remember the price book is a notebook, not a calculator — it remembers your rates, but you still do the billing math yourself.

Owner Contributions & Security Deposit Allocation

Two money-in patterns demonstrated on the Lepi call that belong in the Week 9 repertoire:

Historical owner contributions

To enter owner contributions manually (for example, historical funds that shouldn't ride in on a balance transfer): Accounting → Money In → green actions → Record Owner Receipt — choose the portfolio, enter amount, reference and posting date, then check it off as a deposit once the funds are actually moved.

Splitting a portal payment that included a security deposit

When a tenant pays first month's rent and security deposit together through the portal, everything lands in the property-management checking account. The demonstrated fix: a virtual bank transfer under Banking → green actions to move the deposit portion to the security deposit account, linked to the unit — no journal entry required. Crucially, the in-system transfer doesn't move real money: the matching physical transfer between actual bank accounts must be executed manually.

The bank transfer inside Rentvine is bookkeeping, not banking. Record the transfer in the system so the ledgers are right, then go move the actual dollars between the actual accounts — both halves, every time.

Payout Hygiene: NACHA Speed & Two Reset Rules

From the Lee Connor training and the Morning Training, payout details that go beyond what's already in the Week 12 material:

  • NACHA timing: standard owner distribution processing runs two to three business days, but NACHA can land same-day or next-day — at the cost of extra steps: generate a test file first, then for each payout batch use actions → generate the NACHA file and manually upload it in the bank's own portal.
  • NACHA cost: NACHA files upload to the bank with no transaction fee, while e-checks cost roughly a dollar each — a real consideration at volume.
  • Reset rule #1 — overdraft override: after using an overdraft override to let a portfolio go temporarily negative for an emergency bill, zero it back out once funds are replenished. Left in place, it quietly permits future negative balances nobody intended.
  • Reset rule #2 — payout bank override: a one-time owner-payout bank-account override (three-dots menu, confirmed with an email authentication code) must be reverted to the default account after the exception run — otherwise every future payout follows the exception.
Overrides are loans you make against your future attention — the overdraft override and the payout bank override both keep working until someone remembers to put them back. Make the reset part of the same task, not a someday task.

Screening & Applications: Credit Freezes and Template Rules

From the Morning Training, three application-side rules to teach:

  • The 24-hour credit-freeze rule: after an applicant unfreezes their Equifax credit, screening cannot be successfully rerun until a full 24 hours after the exact time of Equifax's acceptance/confirmation email. Unfrozen at 3:28 PM yesterday means rerun after 3:28 PM today — not this morning. If screening still fails after the full 24 hours, the applicant should contact Equifax directly.
  • Resending a rental verification: when landlord details need correcting, open the applicant's application, void the current address in the rental-history section, add a new rental history with the corrected details, and resend. (The system also auto-resends landlord verification reminders every three days — voiding is for when you can't wait.)
  • Templates are not retroactive: editing an application template after it's been sent does not update applications already issued — the applicant must be sent a brand-new application. The minimum rental-history requirement count stays as set; applicants can always submit more than the minimum.
Credit unfreezes are on Equifax's clock, not ours: a full 24 hours from the confirmation email, to the minute, before rescreening. And if you change an application template, you haven't changed anyone's application — you've changed the next one you send.

Support-Side Tracing: PRNC Codes & Notification Limits

For trainees headed into support roles, the Morning Training demonstrated the internal tracing workflow for payment questions ("why did Forte take $1,600 out of my account?"):

  • Every individual transaction carries a PRNC_ code — paste it into Forte to see that exact transaction as it happened at the processor. The settlement reference (top-left of settlement details) identifies the whole settlement batch the same way. In the real case, a $1,600 bank withdrawal resolved cleanly into two returned payments shown side-by-side in the money-out view.
  • When a settlement's money-in/money-out lines don't sum to the settlement total, the path is: pull the settlement in the processor, identify the missing transaction, and file a database escalation to tier 2 — this is a dev-team fix, not something to adjust locally.
  • There is no audit trail for automated notifications — the system sends hundreds of thousands daily and doesn't store a per-notification log. Set expectations honestly: offer a tier-2 feature request, and for personal notifications teach the reliable pattern — be the primary assignee on anything you need to be notified about, since lease-assignee notifications can be prioritized to a different person.
  • "My invoice disappeared": invoices stay in Accounting → Money Out → Invoices until converted to a bill or rejected — they don't go anywhere else. A "lost" invoice was almost always converted to a bill that was then voided.
A tenant's bank statement and Rentvine's ledger meet at the PRNC code — trace the code, and "why did money leave my account" turns into two specific returned payments you can screenshot. No guessing, no vibes, just the transaction trail.

Closing

If one theme runs through October 8th, it's that Rentvine is honest about what it can't undo: submitted e-checks, processed screenings, sent applications. The craft is in front-loading the care — verify the amount, check the lease status, search for the bill, confirm the 24 hours have passed — and in knowing the small set of reversible fixes (post-date edits, profile deactivation, deleting a bad recurring credit) when something does land wrong. Teach the finality first and the fixes second, and trainees will develop exactly the right instincts.

The system gives you one cheap moment to be careful — before you hit post. Everything after that costs more. Spend the cheap moment.
